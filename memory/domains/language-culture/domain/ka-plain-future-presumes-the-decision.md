## In a request to an authority the Georgian plain future states the recipient's decision as made; ask with a conditional, state intent with the future participle

In a text to an authority, the Georgian plain future turns an English request or intention into a fact. "წარმოგიდგენთ … 9–13 ნოემბერს" ("we will present to you … 9–13 November") and "ქუთაისში დაიწყება" ("it will begin in [redacted]") tell a mayor that the meeting and the city's decision are already settled. This happened twice in one deck ([redacted]-mayor s06.subtitle-01 and s06.t-02, 2026-10-27; reflection 2026-W44, messages 01a17d3a, 01a17d3b, 01a17d3c, 01a17d3d). The cold reader flagged the first ("states the dates but asks nothing"), and brand-comms' comparison with the English found both.
Two forms for two jobs. A request takes the conditional: "მოხარული ვიქნებით, თუ … საშუალებას მოგვცემთ" ("we would be glad if you gave us the opportunity …"). An intention that does not presume the decision takes the future participle: "პირველად ქუთაისში დასანერგი" ("to be introduced first in [redacted]"). A title presumes too: პიონერი ("pioneer") gives the city a name it has not accepted.
Why it slips through: the Georgian future is also the polite form of a promise, so it sounds natural to the author, and the sentence is grammatical, so the first pass rarely sees it.
How to apply: in every text to an authority or a partner (deck, letter, invitation), at the second pass compare each future verb whose outcome rests with the recipient against the source's modality: a request becomes a conditional, an intention becomes the სა-…-ი participle.
